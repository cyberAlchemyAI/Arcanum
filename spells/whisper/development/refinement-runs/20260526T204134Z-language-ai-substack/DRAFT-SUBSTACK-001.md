# The Power Of Language As Personal Code

_First reviewable draft for the Arcanum research group._

There is a strange moment that happens when you give a process a name.

A loose habit becomes easier to notice. A repeated frustration becomes something you can point at. A half-formed way of working becomes shareable: "this is the move I make when an idea is still foggy," or "this is the pattern I use when a conversation needs to become a plan." Naming does not solve the work, but it gives the work a handle.

This is why Yuval Noah Harari's `Sapiens` is useful here. Not because we need one more grand theory, but because its shared-fiction frame points at something practical: humans coordinate through stories, symbols, and imagined structures that enough people can hold together. If language helps us organize behavior around those structures, generative AI changes who gets to shape them.

For a long time, most people inherited the symbolic systems around them. The language of software belonged to engineers. The language of science belonged to trained specialists. The language of law, finance, bureaucracy, publishing, therapy, strategy, and art all came with their own gates and rituals. To enter a domain, you had to learn its syntax. Often that was necessary. But it also meant that many people could feel their own thinking was only legitimate after it had been translated into someone else's authorized form.

Generative AI does not remove the need for rigor. It does not make natural language magically equivalent to software. It does something subtler and, I think, more interesting: it lets more people operate on language as a tool-making medium.

You can name a pattern. You can turn that name into a repeatable instruction. You can test whether the instruction works. You can revise it. You can create a schema for it, a shape that says: here are the inputs, here is the transformation, here is the output, here is how we know whether it helped. You can give that schema to another person, or to an AI collaborator, and ask it to run, critique, adapt, or extend the pattern.

That is close to what code has always done, but it is not the same thing as code. Software code is executed by machines with strict syntax. Personal symbolic code is executed across people, tools, memory, attention, and repeated practice. It is softer, but not vague. It can still have names, constraints, tests, interfaces, and failure modes.

This is the frame I keep returning to while working on Arcanum.

Arcanum is not important here as a product pitch. It is useful as a live example of a research practice where language is being shaped into tools. We create names for workflows. We use aliases as handles for patterns we want to repeat. We build schemas so a process does not disappear back into a long explanation every time we need it.

An alias, in this sense, is not just a shortcut. It is workflow capture.

When I say `refine`, I do not only mean "make this better." I mean a particular kind of movement: take a vague idea, locate its target, build the smallest useful seed, test the shape, expose the risks, and decide whether it is ready for execution or needs more design. The word becomes a handle for a process.

When I say `invoke`, I do not only mean "call something." I mean: turn intent into governed artifacts. Define what is being asked. Design the shape. Produce a plan or handoff that another process can execute. Again, the name compresses a whole workflow.

When I say `whisper`, I am experimenting with a writing-specific version of this pattern. A whisper is not just a prompt for text. It is a way to shape writing intent: audience, tone, transport, narrative anchor, body parts, constraints, validation checks, and the residue we want the reader to carry away.

This matters because most creative work breaks down when the process stays invisible. We say "write a post" or "make it inspiring" or "explain the idea," and then we wonder why the result feels generic. The missing layer is often not talent. It is structure. What is the piece trying to do to the reader? What kind of public is it speaking to? What emotional residue should remain? What body parts does this transport type require? What must be avoided?

Once those questions have names, they become movable. They become things we can inspect, test, remix, and teach.

This is where schemas enter.

A schema is a shape for thinking and action. It says: these are the fields that matter. For a text, the schema might include objective, target public, tone, narrative anchor, transport type, body parts, evidence policy, and validation checks. For a workflow, it might include trigger, inputs, transformation, tools, outputs, quality bar, and learning residue.

A meta-schema is one level higher. It is a shape for making shapes. For example, a simple public-facing meta-schema for any recurring creative workflow might be:

1. Name the pattern.
2. Say when it should be used.
3. List its inputs.
4. Describe the transformation it performs.
5. Define the output.
6. Name the checks that prove it worked.
7. Capture what should be learned for the next run.

That is not software engineering syntax. A writer could use it. A teacher could use it. A founder could use it. A researcher could use it. A small group could use it to turn one good conversation into a repeatable practice.

The exciting part is not that AI writes the words for us. The exciting part is that AI lets us negotiate with our own symbolic systems. We can ask: is this name doing the right work? Is this schema too private? Does this alias compress the workflow without hiding the judgment? Is this metaphor carrying more than it can responsibly hold? What happens if this technique moves from a Substack post to a fundraising note, a slide deck, a book chapter, or a workshop?

That last question matters because different transports need different bodies. A Substack post can afford wonder, context, and invitation. Fundraising copy needs urgency, trust, proof, and a path to action. A slide deck needs sequence, contrast, and retention. A book needs architecture. The same underlying idea cannot simply be poured into every container. It needs to be recomposed.

This is why I think language with AI becomes a kind of personal code. Not because every sentence is executable in the software sense, and not because metaphor should replace precision. It becomes code when it lets a person or group encode a repeatable way of seeing, choosing, making, and checking.

The democratizing possibility is real. A person no longer has to wait until their thinking is translated into official software, official science, official strategy, or official jargon before it can become operational. They can start by naming what they already do. Then they can shape that name into a workflow. Then they can ask AI to help stress-test, formalize, adapt, and reuse it.

The danger is also real. Personal symbolic systems can become private languages. They can become beautiful but unusable. They can become a fog of names that feel powerful to insiders and opaque to everyone else. That is why translation matters. Every alias should be able to unfold back into plain language. Every schema should be able to show an example. Every powerful name should remain answerable to the work it claims to do.

Maybe this is the new literacy I am circling around: not merely prompt literacy, and not merely coding literacy, but symbolic tooling literacy. The ability to shape language into reusable tools while keeping those tools legible, testable, and alive.

Harari's frame gives us the long arc: humans coordinate through shared stories. Generative AI gives us a new workshop inside that ancient capacity. Arcanum is one attempt to build there deliberately, with names, aliases, schemas, and validation instead of vibes alone.

The question I want to leave with is small enough to try:

What is one workflow in your life that deserves a name?

Not a task. Not a productivity hack. A pattern you already repeat, maybe badly, maybe intuitively. How would you name it? What would its inputs be? What would count as a good output? What would you want it to teach you each time it runs?

That name might be the beginning of your own code.
