# When the Shim Is Not the Fix

The desk fit the wall, held the books, and reached the right height. It also
moved every time someone typed.

That exactness mattered. "The desk is wrong" would have sent us wandering.
"The desk moves sideways under typing force" gave us somewhere to begin.

The floor was uneven, so we tried the cheapest visible repair: a thin shim
under the shortest foot. The top became level.

Then we pushed the corner again.

The sideways wobble remained.

The shim had fixed the contact between one foot and the floor. It had not
changed the way the legs joined the top. We had repaired a real problem, just
not the problem that was making the desk sway.

The original plan had hidden all of that work inside three words: "attach the
legs." Those words now had to carry joint shape, rail size, screw position,
wood thickness, and a way to test sideways force. We could keep stuffing new
instructions into the same step, but the step would stop telling us who owned
which decisions or how to know they were finished.

So we drew a smaller job.

It would receive the desk height, attachment points, available wood, and
expected load. It would produce one leg-and-rail assembly. On the workbench, it
had to match its dimensions, keep its joints tight, and resist a sideways push.
Back in the desk, it still had to preserve the fit, height, load, and stability
promises of the whole build.

The smaller job did not earn independence merely because we drew a box around
it. First it had to work.

The first test joint split when a screw sat too close to the edge. That failure
was cheaper on a scrap piece than on four finished legs. We moved the screw,
made another joint, and pushed from the side. The second one held.

Two assemblies followed. Their dimensions matched. Their joints stayed tight.
They resisted sideways force on the workbench.

That was one pass, not two.

The assemblies returned to the desk. We attached the top, moved it back to the
one-meter wall, loaded it with the laptop and books, and repeated every original
check.

Fit passed. Height passed. Load passed. Level passed. Stability passed.

The smaller build had now proved two things: it worked on its own, and it worked
inside the desk that needed it. The wobble was gone. A small screw mark remained
where the old corner joint had been removed, but appearance was not blocking
this bedroom desk.

The residue was lower, the important promises passed, and the result had
returned to the whole. The smaller build had earned its boundary as a **craft
layer**. Its successful return was **recomposition**.

This is what keeps smaller work from turning into endless fragmentation. A
piece is not valuable because it is small. It is valuable when it carries a
coherent responsibility, can be checked, and returns something the larger work
can use.

The desk was not perfect. It was steady enough for the job we had chosen.

So we stopped.
