# The Wobble We Chose

For fifteen seconds, the screen held only a table.

Then came the question: what would you need to build this?

Wood. Screws. A saw. Measurements. A plan. The list grew until someone asked
the question that changed all the others: what kind of table?

A dining table and a bedroom desk do not need the same dimensions, carry the
same weight, or live in the same room. So this one became a small desk for a
narrow bedroom. It had to fit a one-meter wall, hold a laptop and two books,
and stay steady while someone typed. The builder had a drill, a hand saw, basic
lumber, and one afternoon. The floor was old and slightly uneven.

Now there was a real choice.

The fast version used four legs attached directly at the corners. The steadier
version added side rails, but took longer to cut and join. Both fit the wall.
Both looked reasonable in a sketch. With one afternoon and basic tools, the
fast version won.

Choosing speed did not erase the rest of the promise. The desk still had to fit
the wall, reach the right height, hold the expected weight, and remain steady
while typing.

Those four promises gave us something a vague phrase like "a good desk" never
could: a future disagreement we could settle. In Craft, that chosen shape of a
valid result is called a **schema**.

The schema did not say what happened first. We still had to measure, mark, cut,
attach, place, and test. The order told us how to work. The schema told us what
the work still owed us when it was done.

Soon the drawing became a desk. It stood against the wall with a laptop and two
books on top. The sketch had become something reality could challenge: an
**artifact**.

It fit. The height was right. It carried the load.

Three promises had evidence. One did not.

A hand pressed the front corner.

The desk moved sideways.

It did not collapse. It was not useless. It simply failed one promise that had
been visible before the build. Comparing the desk with those earlier promises
was **validation**, and the wobble was the meaningful mismatch left behind:
**residue**.

The wobble had a history. We had chosen corner joints without side rails. We
knew the floor was uneven. We had kept stability as a requirement, but we had
not yet seen what typing force would do to the finished structure.

That does not make the earlier choice foolish. It makes the result informative.
We chose with the evidence we had, built something inspectable, and let reality
show us what the sketch could not.

Now the desk asks a better question than "Is it good?"

Can this wobble be closed with a small repair, or is "attach the legs" hiding a
project of its own?
